MODULE 01 / SESSION 01  ·  SAFETY, HEALTH & ENVIRONMENT

The Foreman's
Call Sheet

Two scenarios. Both about other crews. You'll work through them in your group of three — first on your own, then together. There are no perfect answers. Only the call you would make.

RUN TIME 30–35 MIN GROUP SIZE 3 MATERIALS 1 LAPTOP / GROUP SUBMISSION MICROSOFT FORMS
Before you start

Why we're doing this

Sure Steel foremen know how to run their own crew. The harder call is what to do when somebody else's crew is doing something unsafe — and nobody's stopping them. That's the gap this session is built around.

You've all read about Steve Halverson going to that wife's door. You've read Boyd Worsham finding the laborer 35 feet below the skylight. You know what's at stake. The question isn't do you care. The question is: when it's not your guy, do you still call it?

STEP 01
Read
Read the scenario together. Don't rush. Look for what's said and what's not.
STEP 02
Decide solo
Each of you answers the four decision points on your own first. No talking.
STEP 03
Argue it out
Compare your answers. Where do you agree? Where do you split? Who has the strongest case?
STEP 04
Submit one answer
As a group, agree on one consensus call for each decision. Submit it through the form.
01
Scenario One
10 minutes · 4 decision points
Briefing · Job #4218

The Decking Crew Next Door

Project 6-story office, downtown Your role Raising gang foreman Time 10:42 AM, Tuesday Weather 81°F, light wind

You're running the raising gang on the 5th floor of a 6-story office building downtown. Steel is going up clean — your crew is on schedule and safe. The decking sub on the floor below you is a different company, working under the same GC.

Twenty minutes ago, your connector Diego came down off the iron and pulled you aside. He says the decking foreman just told his guys to stop tying off because they're "only here another two hours and the harnesses are slowing us down." Diego watched two of their guys walk the perimeter — about 22 feet up — with no fall protection. One of them was carrying a sheet of decking by himself.

You walked over to the edge yourself and confirmed it. The GC superintendent is on the ground floor and hasn't said anything. Their guys are still working unprotected as you're standing there.

"I won't tell if you won't tell. We're almost done."
— What the decking foreman reportedly said to his crew.

What you know

  • OSHA requires fall protection at 6 feet in construction. They're at 22 feet.
  • The decking crew works for a different sub. You don't have authority over their foreman.
  • Your crew is on schedule. Stopping anything costs your production.
  • The GC has a published Stop Work policy that says "any worker, any company, any time."
  • You've never met the decking foreman before today.
!

Your call

Each foreman answers these on their own. Then compare in your group.
1
Is this your problem? Why or why not?
They're not your crew. They're not your company. The GC hasn't asked you to do anything. Walk through your reasoning out loud.
2
Who do you talk to first — and why that person?
Your super. The GC super. The decking foreman. The decking crew directly. There's a right order — and the wrong first call can blow up your standing on the job for the rest of the project.
3
What exact words do you use?
Don't write a paragraph. Write the actual sentence. The thing you'd say if you were standing in front of him right now. (Hint: re-read what the superintendent said to the CEO about boots.)
4
If nothing changes in 10 minutes, what do you do?
Now you've talked to someone and they brushed you off. The guys are still up there. Your next move says everything about what kind of foreman you are.

02
Scenario Two
10 minutes · 4 decision points
Briefing · Job #4218

The Bolt-Up They Skipped

Project Multi-prime, 4-story tech HQ Your role Detail crew foreman Time 7:18 AM, Friday Weather 64°F, clear

You're running a detail crew — bolt-ups, plumb-and-align — on a 4-story tech HQ. There's another ironworking sub on the job working a different scope, ahead of you on the same floor sequence. They erected and bolted up the columns last week. Your crew is now moving in to finish the connections behind them.

You walk the 4th floor at 7:18 AM doing your morning hazard check. At a column splice on gridline C-7, you notice the inspection tag is missing. The bolts look installed but they're sitting proud — not fully tensioned. You torque-check one with a hand wrench and it turns. Way more than it should.

You ask one of their journeymen who walks by. He shrugs:

"The foreman said we were good. The inspector signed off Wednesday. Don't worry about it."

You can't find the inspection report. You text your super — no answer yet, he's in a GC meeting. Your crew is staged, tools out, ready to start working off these connections in 30 minutes. The connection at C-7 is structural — it ties into a transfer girder.

What you know

  • Bolted connections require documented inspection per the project quality plan and AISC standards.
  • An untensioned slip-critical bolt in a transfer girder splice is a structural defect, not just a punch list item.
  • The other sub's foreman is senior to you — 25 years on, well-respected on this project.
  • If your crew sits idle, the GC will ask why. If you raise a flag and you're wrong, you've called out a senior foreman in front of everyone.
  • OSHA is the floor, not the ceiling. The project quality plan exceeds OSHA on inspections.
!

Your call

Solo first. Then argue it out as a group.
1
What is your immediate next move — in the next 60 seconds?
Before you call anyone. Before you talk to anyone. What is the very first thing you do with your hands and your feet?
2
Who do you call — and in what order?
Your super (still in the meeting). The GC. The third-party inspector. The other sub's foreman. Order matters. Justify your sequence.
3
Your crew is staged and ready. What do you do with them?
Start them on something else. Send them home. Hold them on the clock. Each call costs the company differently — and signals something different to your crew.
4
The other foreman calls you. He says you're "making him look bad." How do you respond — exact words?
Not a paragraph. The actual sentence. This is the moment most foremen get wrong. You can be right and still wreck the working relationship. Or you can hold the line without making an enemy.
